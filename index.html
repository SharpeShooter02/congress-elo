Congressional Trading ELO

Every disclosed trade is scored as a head-to-head match against the S&P 500: a buy that beats the market wins, and a sell made before the stock lags the market wins too. Ratings replay chronologically from 1500, weighted by how reliable each member's record is. 1500 is the median member. Higher means they beat the market more, and more consistently, than most of Congress.

Data: Congress Trading Monitor (House Clerk · Senate eFD · OGE disclosures) · committees from congress-legislators · sectors from US-Stock-Symbols. Prices vs the S&P 500.

Methodology & hosting

The match. Each disclosed trade's return is compared to the S&P 500's over the same period, giving the excess return, blended across the ~30-day, ~1-year, and full holding windows and time-decayed so fast moves count most and slow buy-and-holds count least. For a Purchase, positive excess = win; for a Sale, the stock lagging the market afterward = win. ±0.5% is a tie.

Rating update. ELO with the market fixed at 1500: E = 1/(1+10^((1500−R)/700)), margin multiplier m = min(1+ln(1+|excess%|), 4.5), and R += 32·m·(S−E) with S = 1/0.5/0. Decisions replay in chronological order. The Buys/Sells columns split each record by direction, so a mediocre buyer who sells with suspicious timing is visible at a glance.

One decision, one match. Repeat trades in the same stock and direction within 7 days are collapsed into a single decision. A member filing four purchases of the same stock in a week made one call, and scoring it four times let a single decision dominate a rating. In this dataset that merges about 15% of trades. Broad-market index funds are excluded (S&P 500 and total-market trackers, plus levered and inverse versions): their return is the benchmark, so scoring them as "beat the S&P" is circular. Sector and country funds are kept, since those are real directional bets.

Rating sensitivity. The K-factor is 8, not chess's 32. At 32 a single decision could move a rating by up to 144 points, so the published number tracked whatever happened most recently. It correlated 0.85 with a member's last 50 decisions but only 0.61 with their whole record, and a typical career swung 668 points between peak and trough. That is also why average excess return looked disconnected from the rating: one is a full-sample mean, the other was a recency-weighted random walk. Dropping K to 8 raises the measured reliability from 0.62 to 0.79.

Reliability weighting. Splitting each member's decisions into odd/even halves and correlating the two independent ratings shows how much of a rating is skill rather than luck. Ratings are shrunk toward 1500 by n/(n+27), so a thin record cannot buy a high rating on a lucky streak. Hovering a rating shows how much of it is signal.

The scale. Raw ratings pile up: the middle half of members sat within 46 points of one another while a few outliers ran from 455 to 2288, which makes the number useless for telling most people apart. The shrunk ratings are therefore mapped onto a normal curve by rank, so order is preserved exactly while the pack spreads evenly, so equal rating gaps mean equal differences in standing. 1500 is the median member; ±150 is roughly the 84th/16th percentile. This gives up the literal "expected score against a 1500-rated market" reading, which the shrunk rating no longer supported anyway.

People, not seats. Someone who moves between offices, such as a senator appointed to the cabinet or a representative elected to the Senate, is one trader with one rating. Every role they filed under is listed on their profile.

Sharp calls 🚩. A trade is flagged a sharp call when it beat the S&P by 15%+ within about a month of the disclosure: fast, decisive correctness of the kind non-public information tends to produce (e.g. a Department of Energy official selling an energy stock right before it slid). The 🚩 next to a name counts how many they've made. Fast wins also come from skill, luck, or public news, so a flag is a reason to look closer rather than a finding of wrongdoing.

Data flow. build_leaderboard.py (run in GitHub Actions) pulls the open Congress Trading Monitor dataset, covering House, Senate, and executive-branch filings, computes the ELO, and writes data.json + data.js. This page fetches data.json when hosted (Reload pulls the latest), falling back to data.js for local opens or synthetic sample data.
